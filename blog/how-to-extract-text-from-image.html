How to Extract Text from an Image (5 Easy Methods in 2026)

2026-08-07 · 7 min read

Ever taken a photo of a document, received a screenshot full of text, or found an image online with a quote you wanted to copy — only to realize you can't select the text? You're not alone. Extracting text from an image is one of the most common digital tasks, and thanks to modern OCR technology, it takes seconds.

In this guide, we'll cover five easy ways to extract text from any image, and share tips to get near-perfect accuracy.

Method 1: Use a Free Online Picture to Text Converter (Fastest)

The quickest way for most people is a browser-based OCR tool like our own picture to text converter. Here's how:

  1. Open the converter in any browser — no installation or account needed.
  2. Drag in your image, click to upload it, or simply paste a screenshot with Ctrl+V.
  3. Select the language of the text in your image.
  4. Wait a few seconds while the OCR engine reads the picture.
  5. Copy the extracted text or download it as a .txt file.

A good online converter handles JPG, PNG, WEBP and other formats, supports multiple languages, and — importantly — processes the image in your browser so private documents stay private.

Method 2: Google Lens

Google Lens, built into the Google app and Chrome, can select text inside images. Right-click an image in Chrome and choose "Search with Google Lens", then tap the text to copy it. On phones, point your camera at printed text and Lens will highlight what it can read. It's convenient for quick snippets, though it requires a Google account for some features and sends the image to Google's servers.

Method 3: Your Phone's Built-In Tools

Modern phones have OCR built in. On iPhone, the "Live Text" feature lets you select text directly in the Photos app or Camera. On many Android phones, Google Photos offers "Copy text from image". These are great for casual use, but they can struggle with multi-column layouts and offer little control over language settings.

Method 4: Desktop Software

Traditional programs like Adobe Acrobat (for scanned PDFs) and dedicated OCR suites offer batch processing and advanced layout retention. They are powerful but usually paid, and overkill if you just need to grab text from a few pictures.

Method 5: Microsoft OneNote or Word

OneNote has a hidden gem: right-click any inserted image and choose "Copy Text from Picture". It works surprisingly well for printed text, though accuracy varies with image quality and it's only available if you already use Microsoft's ecosystem.

Tips for Maximum OCR Accuracy

  • Use high-resolution images. Text should be crisp when you zoom in — blurry text confuses every OCR engine.
  • Keep text horizontal. Straighten tilted photos before converting.
  • Good lighting matters. Avoid shadows and glare when photographing documents.
  • Plain backgrounds win. Text over busy images or patterns is harder to read.
  • Pick the right language. Telling the OCR engine the correct language dramatically improves results.

Which Method Should You Choose?

For everyday use — screenshots, photos of pages, receipts, notes — a free online pic to text converter is the fastest and most flexible option: nothing to install, works on every device, and supports batch conversion. Reserve desktop software for large professional scanning projects.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can I extract text from an image for free?

Yes — browser-based tools like PicToTexts are completely free with no daily limits, and because processing happens on your device, free doesn't mean your images are being collected.

What image quality do I need?

As a rule of thumb, each line of text should be at least 20–30 pixels tall in the image. If you can read the text comfortably at 100% zoom, OCR can too.

Can I extract text from multiple images at once?

Yes. PicToTexts accepts up to 10 images per batch and combines the results in order — handy for multi-page documents and slide decks.

Does extraction work in languages other than English?

Modern OCR supports dozens of languages. Select the matching language before converting — it's the single biggest accuracy lever for non-English text.
